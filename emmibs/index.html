Every motion metric is blind to something

Abstract

PROBLEM

Evaluating a learned manipulation policy currently means running it on the robot. Robot time is the scarcest resource in a training pipeline, and a rollout returns a single bit, success or failure, that does not say whether the plan, the grasp or the motion itself was at fault.

SOLUTION

Φ judges the motion a policy commands before the robot executes it. It is training-free: a robot model and two constants per joint measured on the bench are all it needs; nothing is simulated, learned or tuned by hand. Each action chunk becomes five terms, scored by how unusual the vector is against human demonstrations of the same task, with the responsible term named. Under injected spikes, jitter and chunk-boundary seams, every single metric we test misses at least one class; Φ misses none, in 1.55 ms per chunk.

01 · INTRODUCTION

We built Φ: a scorer that ranks a policy's commanded trajectory before the robot executes it.

THIS PAGE IS A PLAIN-LANGUAGE WALK THROUGH THE CORE OF THE METHOD. EVERY DETAIL IS IN THE PAPER.

It reads the commands, not the outcome. Each action chunk becomes five numbers referenced to what the arm can actually do, and one distance from how humans drive the same task. The rest of this page shows how, what it catches that other metrics miss, and where it stops.

02 · HOW Φ WORKS

A learned policy emits its actions in chunks. Φ sits between the policy and the servos and scores each chunk before it moves anything.

One inference step, left to right: the policy turns its observations into a block of joint targets; Φ reduces that block to five terms referenced to what the arm can do, asks how unusual the vector is against human demonstrations of the same task, and returns a score with its cause. Pass, and the chunk executes. Flag, and it can be dropped, resampled or logged with the term that failed.

Where Φ sits: on the command path, between the policy and the servos. Drawn for a VLA, the case it was built and tested on; any imitation-learned policy that commands joint targets goes in the same slot. What happens inside Φ is explained below.

03 · INSIDE Φ

A chunk becomes five numbers. Φ is how far those five sit from the way humans drive the same arm.

The distance splits exactly into five signed shares, so the score always comes with the term that caused it.

R
Dither ratio: how often the command reverses relative to how fast it gets anywhere. Human ≈ 0.5.
fracEbw
Share of command energy above the servo's corner frequency: heat and vibration, not motion.
p99v
99th-percentile joint speed over the chunk; effectively the maximum.
p99a
99th-percentile acceleration. Calm for most of its length and violent once is dangerous. Human ≈ 17.
seamv
The jump at the chunk boundary as a fraction of the joint's measured top speed. The only term that reads outside its chunk.

Four terms describe the shape of the motion, one the join to the previous chunk. Each is referenced to the arm's measured top speed and rate constant, so the same score means the same thing on any joint.

04 · THE REFERENCE

Humans set the reference. A replayed demonstration shows what a good score looks like.

Before deployment, the terms are computed for every chunk of the teleoperated demonstrations of the task, on the same arm. Their mean μ and covariance Σ are the reference, and Φ is the distance from it, counted in σ: how much humans themselves vary on each term.

0 σ
Exactly the human average for that term.
±1 σ
Ordinary. Two thirds of human chunks live inside this band.
+3 σ
Almost no human chunk gets here. The sign is the direction: + is more than humans do, − is less.

Φ combines the terms into one distance and splits it back into per-term shares, so a high score always comes with the term and the σ that caused it.

Distance is not a verdict. A practice we found useful, and recommend: before reading any policy, score a trajectory you trust. Replay a recorded demonstration on the robot and score it like a rollout; we call it the oracle. Its Φ per chunk and its three summaries are the yardstick a policy's rollout is read against. Φ says the arm was never driven like this by a person, and names the reason; whether that matters for the task is your call.

06 · RESULTS

Every single metric we tested misses at least one defect class. Φ misses none, on both arms.

Three defects were injected into held-out human episodes at magnitudes from 0.25° to 32°, doubling each step: an isolated command spike, high-frequency jitter, and a seam, a jump at the boundary between two chunks. ε50 is the smallest magnitude at which a detector flags half of the episodes. Each of the five terms alone, and the smoothness scalars SPARC and LDLJ, leaves at least one class undetected on at least one arm. Φ reads all five at once and detects all three on both arms, with no per-arm tuning beyond the two bench constants.

07 · SETTING Φ UP ON YOUR ARM

Two bench measurements per joint and one pass over your demonstrations. Nothing is learned and nothing is tuned by hand.

Everything Φ needs at inference is fixed before deployment, once per arm and task. The steps below are what the paper did for the SO-101 and the RoArm-M3 Pro; the same recipe applies to any joint-controlled arm with a teleoperated dataset.

1
Measure the joints

Two numbers per joint, read off the hardware. A ramp test gives the top speed q̇peak: command a long move at full rate and take the steady velocity. A step test gives the rate constant: command a small step, fit the response as dead time plus a first-order lag, and keep the time constant τ; the bandwidth fbw = 1 / (2πτ) follows from it. Repeat once per joint.

RAMP TEST
q̇peak,j
top speed the servo can hold, one long move
STEP TEST
τj → fbw,j
dead time + first-order fit to a small step; the RoArm shows about 110 ms of dead time
ROBOT MODEL
URDF → J(q)
the arm's URDF: joint order, limits, and the Jacobian
2
Point it at your demonstrations

Take the teleoperated episodes you fine-tune on, on the same arm. Cut each into chunks the way your policy emits them, compute the terms for every chunk with the constants from step 1, and keep the mean μ and covariance Σ over all chunks. That pair is the reference. The paper used 200 episodes per platform; the two datasets are public.

3
Score the oracle

Replay one held-out demonstration on the robot and score it like a rollout. Its Φmax, Φbad and Φmean are what good looks like on your hardware; every policy is read against them, as in the demo above. Φ per chunk costs 1.55 ms, so the same code runs offline over a dataset and online in the control loop.

·
What Φ is

A distance from teleoperation, not a certificate of admissibility: a motion humans never make can still be safe, and a human-like one can still fail the task. It reads kinematics, not dynamics, so it knows nothing of payload or contact forces. And it needs no task labels, which also means it cannot tell you whether the task was done.

Bench constants and reference for both arms ship as phi/ref_so101.json and phi/ref_roarm.json, together with the scorer that runs on this page; the recipe for reproducing them from the datasets is in the repository.
